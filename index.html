Takeaways

Three things the record supports.

  1. 01

    Risk is a place, not a sprinkle.

    Incidents cluster on known hillside ground. Large failures sit inside those clusters. Changing the years on the map does not invent a new geography.

  2. 02

    The wet season is the story's clock.

    of incidents and of large failures fall in June–September. June is the single heaviest month, with reports.

  3. 03

    Extreme rain days carry the large failures.

    The share of large incidents stays near 3% until Observatory rainfall exceeds 200 mm, then rises to 14.1%. Temperature was joined to every date and did not separate the record the way that threshold does.

04 · Method

How to read this

Incidents are the CEDD Geotechnical Engineering Office public landslide record, kept where a failure date and a Hong Kong 1980 Grid coordinate were both present, then limited to 2000–2024. Coordinates were transformed to WGS84 for the map.

Rainfall is the Hong Kong Observatory daily total, and temperature is the Observatory daily maximum, both joined on the failure date. That is a city-centre signal. A slope in Sai Kung can fail in a storm that barely registers at the Observatory. Read the rainfall link as context, not as a gauge sitting on the scar.

“Large” means the top 5 percent of recorded failure volume in this window: at least 174 cubic metres. “Minor” volumes in the source file were kept as 0.5 m³ so they would not disappear from the count.

Four variables are on the page: location, time, failure volume, and daily rainfall. Position, colour and size carry them — green heat versus amber dots on the map, green bars versus the blue rain line, and the length of each rainfall bin.
